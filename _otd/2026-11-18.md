On this day in 1886, Arthur Herbert Malins was born in Hastings, Sussex. He would later change his name to Geoffrey Herbert Malins and become a British film director, most famous for his camera and editing work on the 1916 war film The Battle of the Somme. This production combined documentary and propaganda, reaching an audience of over 20 million viewers.

Malins began his career as a photographer, the son of a hairdresser, before securing a position with the Croydon-based Clarendon Film Company in 1910. Prior to the First World War, he joined the British arm of Gaumont. In late 1915, he received a War Office appointment as an official cameraman, receiving an honorary rank and being sent to the front with an assistant. The footage he shot with John McDowell in 1916 formed the basis of The Battle of the Somme, a film that was a huge success.

His work at the front was hampered by increasing ill health, and he was invalided out of the army in June 1918. He was awarded an OBE that same month for his work as official photographer in circumstances of great difficulty and danger. Malins published an account of his wartime filming in 1920 entitled How I Filmed the War. He later founded the Garrick Film Company in 1919 and undertook a motorcycle journey around the world in 1926. He died in Durban in 1940.
